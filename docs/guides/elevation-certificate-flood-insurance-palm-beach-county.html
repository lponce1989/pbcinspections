Elevation certificates in Palm Beach County: when flood insurance needs one and how to find yours

Checked Nov 20, 2026

Not insurance advice. General information only. Your insurer or agent decides what it needs.

Short answer: FEMA's FloodSmart site says an elevation certificate shows how high a building is compared to how high local floodwaters are expected to get, and that most homeowners do not need one to get flood insurance. The exception it names is a home in a high-risk Zone A or high-risk coastal Zone V flood area, where an elevation certificate may be needed to verify that the property complies with local safety standards. Even outside those zones, FloodSmart says it is worth checking whether your property already has one on file, and Palm Beach County publishes certificates it has on file through its public flood website.

Citizens Property Insurance says most of its new and renewing personal residential policies that include wind coverage must also have and maintain flood insurance by Jan 1, 2027. For who that covers and the exemptions, see the Citizens flood insurance requirement. This guide covers what an elevation certificate records, when it matters for the price of flood insurance, how to look for one in Palm Beach County, and who can complete a new one.

What an elevation certificate shows

  • What is on it: FloodSmart says the certificate includes your home's location, characteristics and its First Floor Height, which it defines as the height of the first (lowest) floor above the ground.
  • Basements: FloodSmart says basements do not factor into First Floor Height because they are below ground and measured separately, and that a basement will be noted on the certificate.
  • Why your community keeps them: FEMA says a community's permit file must have an official record showing that new buildings and substantial improvements in Special Flood Hazard Areas are properly elevated. It encourages communities to use FEMA's Elevation Certificate for that record because the property owner can also use it to obtain flood insurance.
  • The form: FEMA says communities in its Community Rating System (CRS) must use the FEMA Online Elevation Certificate, FEMA Form FF-206-FY-22-152. In a July 15, 2026 memo to Write Your Own flood insurers and the NFIP Direct Servicing Agent, the NFIP said that form is under review and that the existing form on FEMA's website should be used until the update is approved.

Do you need one for flood insurance?

FEMA's agent site says that under Risk Rating 2.0, an elevation certificate is no longer required to purchase coverage. FEMA instead uses its own tools and resources to determine a building's first-floor height as one of the factors in the rate, and a property owner may choose to provide an elevation certificate to the agent to see if it lowers the cost of insurance.

FloodSmart puts it this way: elevation certificates do not set your rate, but they can identify discounts. The same agent article says the NFIP will continue to use elevation certificates for floodplain management building requirements, which can affect eligibility for Community Rating System discounts.

Palm Beach County's Building Division says flood insurance is not just for properties in the floodplain, and that more than 25% of flood insurance claims are for structures in Zone X.

How it can change the price

  • Height off the ground: FloodSmart says that the higher the first floor elevation, the less likely a home is to suffer flood damage, and that a home less likely to suffer flood damage will pay less for flood insurance.
  • An example from FEMA: FEMA's agent article says a property owner can save 34% on the premium by elevating the insured building from 0 feet to 5 feet above the ground.
  • Machinery and equipment: the same article says properly elevating machinery and equipment covered under the Standard Flood Insurance Policy can save up to 5% on a premium. FloodSmart's discount tool describes this discount as elevating all covered machinery and equipment to at least the elevation of the floor above the building's first floor.
  • Flood openings: FloodSmart's tool says a policyholder may receive a mitigation discount if the building's enclosure or crawlspace has proper flood openings, or engineered openings with documentation.
  • Estimate first: FloodSmart's flood insurance mitigation discount tool asks for foundation type, First Floor Height and those two mitigation items, then shows an estimated discount. It notes that because a loss and expense constant is applied separately, the difference between two quotes will not exactly match the percentages listed, and that minimum or maximum rates can affect the savings.

Check for one already on file in Palm Beach County

  • The county's public flood website: Palm Beach County's quick reference says its public flood website lets you search a property by address to see its flood zone, Base Flood Elevation and Design Flood Elevation. If documents are on file, it says you can find the elevation certificate on the searchable Elevation Certificate page or on the property's public profile, "saving you the cost of getting a new one."
  • myGeoNav: the county's myGeoNav quick reference says to turn on the Elevation Certificate layer in myGeoNav and search by address or owner name. An orange dot inside the property line means an elevation certificate is on file, and green or red dots show current or expired Letters of Map Amendment and related letters.
  • Confidential addresses: the same quick reference says documents for confidential addresses are not available for display or download, and that redacted copies may be available by request to FloodZone@pbcgov.org.
  • Ask the county: the Building Division's flood page lists FloodZone@pbcgov.org and a Flood Zone Hotline at (561) 233-5374. The county's letter on flood zone information asks you to include the Property Control Number (PCN), which it says is required for vacant lots, the street address, the current owner if known, and your name and contact information.
  • Inside a city: that letter says the Building Division's flood zone information, and copies of elevation certificates, are for properties in unincorporated Palm Beach County. To tell which you are in, it says to check the first two digits of the 17-digit PCN on your tax bill: 00 means unincorporated county, and anything else means a municipality, which you must contact for flood zone or similar information. FloodSmart says local floodplain managers often work in a city's or county's building, permitting, engineering or land use department.

Who completes a new one

If your property does not have an elevation certificate on file, FloodSmart says you will need to hire a land surveyor, engineer or architect to complete one, and suggests asking your flood insurance agent for advice about hiring someone.

Palm Beach County's flood page says the flood zone, elevation certificate and map amendment information on its website is provided strictly as a public service and is subject to change. It says that before buying or building on a property, flood information should always be confirmed with an official flood zone determination from a Florida licensed engineer, architect or surveyor.

Florida's surveyor law is chapter 472. Its definitions section, s. 472.005, refers to the Board of Professional Surveyors and Mappers and the Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services, and defines the practice of surveying and mapping to include measuring and locating elevations.

Elevation certificate vs. wind mitigation inspection

The elevation certificate records flood risk, as described above. The wind mitigation form, OIR-B1-1802, is the uniform mitigation verification form that s. 627.711(2)(a) says all insurers must use when a policyholder submits it for wind insurance discounts.

The same statute says insurers must accept a wind mitigation form signed by a home inspector licensed under s. 468.8314 with the required hurricane mitigation training, a certified building code inspector, a licensed general, building or residential contractor, a professional engineer, a professional architect, or anyone else the insurer recognizes as qualified. Engineers and architects are named both in that statute and on FloodSmart's list for elevation certificates; land surveyors are on FloodSmart's list but are not named in the statute. For the wind side, see who can sign Florida's wind mitigation form. If the certificate shows the home sits higher: map changes

FEMA says that if you believe your property was incorrectly identified as being in a Special Flood Hazard Area, you can ask FEMA for a formal determination of the property's location or elevation relative to that area, called a Letter of Map Change (LOMC). FEMA describes two types:

  • Letter of Map Amendment (LOMA): for an existing structure or parcel on naturally high ground, not elevated by fill, that would not be inundated by the base flood.
  • Letter of Map Revision Based on Fill (LOMR-F): for an existing structure or parcel elevated by earthen fill that would not be inundated by the base flood.

FEMA says homeowners can apply online, that licensed land surveyors and professional engineers can submit LOMA requests through its eLOMA tool, and that if the request is granted, the owner may send the determination to their lender and ask that the federal flood insurance requirement for the structure be removed. Palm Beach County's flood page says its flood team helps residents navigate the LOMC process.

For a Citizens policy with wind coverage, a map change does not end the flood requirement: Citizens' flood page says that for policies with wind coverage on homes outside the Special Flood Hazard Area, the flood requirement applies to all policies regardless of value effective Jan 1, 2027, except condominium unit-owner, tenant content and policies that exclude windstorm or hail coverage.

FAQ

Do I need an elevation certificate to buy flood insurance?

Usually not. FloodSmart says most homeowners do not need one, with the exception of homes in high-risk Zone A or Zone V areas, where one may be needed to verify compliance with local safety standards. FEMA's agent site says an elevation certificate is no longer required to purchase coverage under Risk Rating 2.0.

How do I find out if my Palm Beach County home already has an elevation certificate?

Search your address on the county's public flood website or turn on the Elevation Certificate layer in myGeoNav. You can also email FloodZone@pbcgov.org or call the county's Flood Zone Hotline at (561) 233-5374. If your PCN does not start with 00, the county says to contact your municipality.

Who can complete an elevation certificate?

FloodSmart says to hire a land surveyor, engineer or architect if there is no certificate on file. Palm Beach County says official flood zone determinations should come from a Florida licensed engineer, architect or surveyor.

Will an elevation certificate lower my flood insurance premium?

It might. FEMA's agent site says an owner may provide one to see if it lowers the cost, and FloodSmart says certificates do not set your rate but can identify discounts.

Can my wind mitigation inspector do my elevation certificate?

FloodSmart says to hire a land surveyor, engineer or architect for an elevation certificate. Section 627.711 names engineers and architects among those who can sign the wind mitigation form, along with qualified home inspectors, building code inspectors and contractors, so only an inspector who is also an engineer or architect is on both lists.
